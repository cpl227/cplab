"Is This True?"

A guided tool for stopping an overthinking spiral. Calm the body first, then check the story your mind is telling you against the facts.

Before You Begin

When emotions rise, the mind fills in gaps with assumptions and worst-case scenarios. They can feel completely true, even when the facts don't fully support them. This tool walks through that in order: calm the body first, then gently sort the story from what's actually known, then turn what you find into one small action.

Use it when a thought feels too loud to sit with, before a big reaction, or after a conflict, anytime you want to check whether the story in your head matches what's actually going on.

Step One · Calm the Body

Before you can look at anything clearly, the noise in your head has to come down. Tap one or two to try right now, no need to do them all.

Temperature

Why this helps: cold on the face triggers the body's dive reflex, which slows the heart rate and calms the nervous system quickly, before you try to think your way out of anything.

Movement

Why this helps: 30–120 seconds of intense movement burns off the adrenaline behind that wired, keyed-up feeling. You only need a short burst, not a full workout.

Breath

Why this helps: lengthening the exhale is one of the most direct ways to signal safety to your nervous system. It's a simple switch, not a mood you have to force.

Muscle Release

Why this helps: tensing a muscle hard and releasing it teaches your body the felt difference between holding on and letting go, often more noticeable than trying to "relax" directly.

Step Two · The Noise

Once the body's settled even a little, put the loudest thought into words, exactly how it sounds, no softening, no editing.

Tap for examples
Relationship

"My partner didn't wish me luck before my interview. I must not matter to them."

Work

"My manager didn't reply to my email right away. I must be in trouble."

Friendship

"She didn't like my post. She's probably annoyed with me."

Family

"My mom sounded short on the phone. She must be disappointed in me."

Self-Worth

"I stumbled over my words in the meeting. Everyone thinks I'm incompetent."

Money

"I overspent this week. I'm terrible with money and always will be."

Parenting

"My kid had a meltdown in public. I must be failing as a parent."

Step Three · The Evidence

Now check that thought against what you actually know. Concrete, observable facts only, what a camera could see, not how it felt.

Tap for examples: evidence for
Relationship

"They didn't text me. They were online. I mentioned the interview yesterday."

Work

"It's been six hours with no reply. My last project needed a round of edits."

Friendship

"She usually likes my posts within a day. It's been two days now."

Family

"Her voice was flat. The call was shorter than usual."

Self-Worth

"I did pause a few times. One person glanced at their phone."

Money

"I spent more than I planned to on takeout this week."

Tap for examples: evidence against
Relationship

"They mentioned it was a busy day. They've shown up for me before."

Work

"They're in back-to-back meetings today. They've never flagged my work as a problem."

Friendship

"She's mentioned being busy with a move this week. She replied warmly to my last text."

Family

"She mentioned a headache earlier. She's been short with everyone lately, not just me."

Self-Worth

"Two people nodded along. No one said anything negative afterward."

Money

"I've stuck to my budget the last three weeks. One off week isn't a pattern."

Why this helps: separating what actually happened from the story about what happened is the core move in cognitive processing approaches. Most emotional spirals are fueled by the story we elaborate in our heads, not the facts themselves.

Step Four · The Shift

Weigh both sides above and write the most honest, steady version of what's actually going on. Truth is usually quieter than the noise.

Tap for examples
Relationship

"There was distance today. That doesn't mean I don't matter to them."

Work

"The timing is probably about their schedule, not my performance."

Friendship

"She's likely just distracted right now, not upset with me."

Family

"She's probably not feeling well today, not upset with me specifically."

Self-Worth

"I stumbled a little. That's normal, and it doesn't erase what I know."

Money

"This week was off track. That doesn't undo the weeks I stayed on budget."

Step Five · Turn It Into a Plan

Take the grounded truth from Step Four and turn it into one small, doable thing. Clarity without action tends to slide right back into overthinking.

The most reliable way to do this Build a simple "if this, then that" plan: "If [this happens], I will [small action]." Naming the exact moment and the exact response together is one of the most consistently replicated findings in behavior-change research. It roughly doubles the odds you'll actually follow through, compared to just meaning to (Gollwitzer, 1999).
Example

"If I catch myself replaying this conversation tonight, I'll write down the thought instead of rehearsing it in my head."

Your if-then plan:

Need a starting point, or still not sure what to write? A few, in the same format:

Tap for examples
Relationship

"If I feel anxious after being left on read, I'll send: 'feeling a bit anxious, can we check in for 5 minutes tonight?'"

Work

"If I'm frozen looking at my task list, I'll do the smallest one first, one email, one reply."

Self-Worth

"If I start spiraling about a mistake, I'll name two things I handled well this month."

Conflict

"If someone pushes back on my boundary, I'll repeat it once, plainly, and stop explaining."

Family

"If I feel dismissed on a call home, I'll say it plainly instead of going quiet: 'that landed differently than you probably meant.'"

Friendship

"If I notice myself assuming a friend is upset with me, I'll just ask instead of guessing."

Money

"If I overspend one week, I'll look at the number once, without judgment, and reset the next day."

Parenting

"If my kid melts down in public, I'll remind myself this is a normal part of the age, not a reflection of my parenting."
