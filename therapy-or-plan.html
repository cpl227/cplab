Process or Progress?

What do I need?

Answer eight quick questions, and we'll help you figure out if you need to talk this through, or just take action.

This is a self-reflection tool, not a diagnostic one. It can help you sort a feeling into a next step. It can't rule a clinical concern in or out, see the note at the end.

Hey, it's Bean from the Lab 👋

What this is: a 2-minute sort for when you're spiraling on something and can't tell if you need to talk it out, or just need a plan.

Why it works: researchers have found that people generally cope in two different ways, working the problem directly, or working through the feeling first (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Neither is wrong, they just need different responses. This sorts out which one you're actually in.

What you'll get: eight quick questions, then a clear next step, either a conversation to have or an action to take.

Step One · Name the Spiral

Start by putting words to it. That alone makes it easier to sort.

What's the thing that's actually been looping in your head lately? How long has this been going on? Days, weeks, longer?
Step Two · Rate Each Statement

Rate how true each one is of your situation right now, not how you feel about it being true.

What are we actually measuring?

Plan-Orientation looks for a clear next step, what researchers call problem-focused coping.

Distress / Talk-It-Through looks for whether the feeling itself needs processing first, what's called emotion-focused coping.

Both are normal, well-studied ways of coping. This just helps you notice which one your situation is asking for more right now (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984).

Plan-Orientation
1. This is tied to one specific, identifiable situation, not a bunch of different things at once.
2. I have a reasonably clear idea of what steps would actually resolve this.
3. There's something realistic I could do about this in the next two weeks.
4. Resolving this depends mostly on things I can control myself.
Step Three · Rate Each Statement

Same as before, how true each one is right now.

Distress / Talk-It-Through
5. This shows up across a lot of different situations, not just one.
6. I can't fully pin down why I feel this way, even after thinking about it a lot.
7. This has been interfering with my day-to-day functioning, like sleep, eating, or concentration.
8. The feeling itself is intense enough that it's hard to think past it.

Step Four · If It's a Plan Problem

Your answers lean toward needing a next step more than a conversation. Let's make it specific.

The one thing I'm avoiding or putting off is: The smallest version of dealing with it, this week, is:

Why this helps: breaking a task down to its smallest possible version is the core idea behind behavioral activation, a well-studied approach that treats avoidance itself as what keeps a problem feeling bigger than it is. Acting on a small piece tends to reduce the stuck feeling faster than waiting to feel ready (Jacobson et al., 1996).

I'll do it by (specific day/time):

Why this helps: attaching a concrete day and time turns a vague intention into what researchers call an implementation intention, a simple "if this time comes, then I do this" plan. Studies find this one small step meaningfully increases the odds of actually following through, compared to just intending to do something (Gollwitzer, 1999).

If it helps to say it out loud
  • "I know what I need to do next. I just haven't done it yet, and that's okay."
  • "I don't need to solve the whole thing today. I just need to do the smallest piece."
  • "This has an end point. I'm not stuck forever, I'm stuck right now."
Step Five · If It's a Talk-It-Through Problem

Your answers lean toward wanting a person more than a plan. You don't have to decide on therapy today, a few lower-stakes starting points. Tap anything you'd like to try:

Why this helps: naming a feeling out loud in specific words, rather than staying inside the vague version in your head, is linked to lower emotional intensity almost immediately. Researchers call this affect labeling; brain imaging studies show it calms the brain's threat response even before anything is resolved (Lieberman et al., 2007).

Why this helps: this is the "expressive writing" method, one of the most replicated findings in this area. Writing honestly about something difficult for a short stretch of time is linked to improved mood and lower stress in the weeks after, even with no one else reading it (Pennebaker & Beall, 1986).

Why this helps: the biggest barrier to getting support is usually the size of the first step, not the decision itself. Making the first step this small, just looking, nothing else, lowers the activation energy enough that it actually gets done, which matters more than it sounds like it should.

Why this helps: giving a feeling a short, defined window to resolve on its own, while staying alert to whether it does, is the same logic behind "watchful waiting," an approach used in stepped-care mental health models. It's not about waiting passively; it's treating time itself as one more piece of information.
